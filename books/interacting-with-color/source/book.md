## p004
FOREWORD
Interaction of Color is a masterpiece that fulfills the goal of Josef Albers's life: "to open eyes." His was a straightforward, heartfelt intention: Albers wanted people to further their appreciation of life itself through the joys of seeing. With messianic zeal, he longed for everyone—regardless of education or circumstances—to go with him to the enchanted territory of color performance and to savor the offerings of vision. Albers's revelations were accessible to one and all, for they are timeless and apprehensible universally.
The alchemy presented in Interaction is glorious in its simplicity, its dependability, and the sense of wonder it bestows on us. Colors are not merely what they first appear to be. They change—which is to say that our perception of them changes—according to their settings. A blue that is luminous and ethereal when surrounded by a dark red may seem solid, even brooding, when encased in a pale yellow. An X that looks gray on a yellow background appears to be yellow on a gray background, yet it is always the same exact hue. The miracles are endless.
Exult in the mysteries! Relish the way that there is no one truth. Celebrate the way that just about everything we see depends on context. Albers beckons you with his immense generosity of heart to enter his kingdom of delights.
The first Interaction of Color, published by Yale University Press in 1963, was a large, precious object: produced to be exquisite thanks to the quality of its design and printing and presentation. But its contents were meant to be distributed to the entire world. Soon enough, Interaction began to proliferate: in paperback editions, with its text translated into seventeen different languages, as well as in electronic form. Its theories became influential in the teaching of art. Albers's students became teachers of its ideas to their students, and the number of apostles multiplied. The mutability of hues and the means of demonstrating it became central to the teaching of art on every level.
Yet color transformation is not simply taught; it is experienced directly. Albers's goal was for us to engage in our own explorations. Interaction guides its readers to be activated. And in making that green look one way in one environment and different in another, the participants in this glorious search come to feel the extraordinary happenings acutely.

## p006
INTRODUCTION
Josef Albers (1888-1976) dedicated Interaction of Color, first published in 1963, to his students. It was alongside them that he developed his ideas about color, and it was with them that the exercises that make up the book were given shape. Most of the book's illustrations are reproductions of students' solutions to the problems that Albers put forward in the classroom. Albers began his teaching career in elementary schools and then went on to work with design students at the Bauhaus in Germany and university students at Black Mountain College in North Carolina and Yale University in New Haven, Connecticut. He believed it was more important to ask the right questions than to direct students toward a specific answer. The same is true in this book. While engaging with the exercises, you will likely come up with unique solutions that might even have surprised Albers. His genius as a teacher was to lead students to their own discoveries, which inspired a deeper understanding of the material.
![p006-f1 904x607](images/2b5db90f53f584e94377bb488f7b016066b99acc700280803e5b1f7087e19e57.jpg)
This book sets out to make Interaction of Color accessible to artists, teachers, and learners from all kinds of backgrounds and to hasten the moment when readers are actively experimenting with color. Albers's ideas about color are not a set of precepts or rules but a playful, approachable, and flexible way to explore color and visual perception. Readers will gain more nuanced ways to see color and will be able to talk about color and to appreciate its ever-changing relativity in every aspect of their lives. No memorizing of complicated theories is required, only the simplest of materials and a willingness to experiment.
Albers included twenty exercises in Interaction of Color. The present book focuses on eight essential exercises, with the hope that the reader will then have easier access to the rest. Interaction of Color begins with the relativity of color—how one color can appear very differently when surrounded by different background colors. This is the basis on which the rest of the book is built, and it is naturally where we also begin.

At the heart of both Interaction of Colorand Albers's color course is the concept of color relativity. On the previous page the two small brown areas are identical in color, but they appear very different from one another because of their relationships with the surrounding colors. Focus your eyes on the line where dark blue and yellow meet, while observing the brown squares in your peripheral vision. Does one appear darker than the other? The same image is shown below with the yellow and dark blue removed to show that the brown is a single color, though it appears to be two different colors when it is surrounded by different background colors. Albers framed the idea of color relativity in an exercise that gives students access to the basic concept quickly and allows them to build upon it following their own curiosity. It can be done in many ways. What follows is a description of one possible approach.

## p009
Materials
• Colored paper—the more the better. Albers's students often used a specific brand called Color-aid. Paint swatch samples, wrapping paper, paper from magazines, or construction paper also work well.
![p009-f1 898x687](images/be7f891825b1ec90b060ca99e65d813ae7771d6a49a5a51c0eb6b47f8fc2d48d.jpg)
• Scissors
• White card stock or sturdy paper
• Glue. Glue sticks are good, as is liquid glue.
• A strip of white paper for each student, approximately two inches wide
Having a good selection of colored paper is the best way to start, but don't worry if you can't get your hands on Color-aid or something similar. While Color-aid packets can include as many as 314 colors, having just 2o is enough to begin.
Albers was clear that the experiments in Interaction of Color should be done with paper and not paint. Applying paint to paper evenly with clean lines requires a set of skills and takes time, preventing a student from immediately engaging with color. Further, with a wide variety of colored paper in front of them, students can work quickly and change directions easily. Pieces of paper that are around three by four inches work well, but any size can be used. Materials can be shared. A table of eight students could all work from one large set of colored paper.
Although the exercise described here uses colored paper, don't let a lack of materials hold you back. Wonderful results cau be discovered by experimenting with fabric, digital tools, and any other colorful material, including paint, despite Albers's direction.
Once you've started, you will see color possibilities all around you!

## p010
Getting started
![p010-f1 785x595](images/80eff710b0169367eaca9d8f2e2e6ac7a479bfc61ce73d908098a4b8fc873578.jpg)
Spread the papers out so that you can see as many colors as possible at the same time. Choose sets of three colors, arranging them in the orientation used in the demonstration to the right (two vertical pieces in the background with one horizontal across the middle). Place a vertical white strip on top so that the horizontally placed color is visually divided and can be seen interacting separately with the two background colors. Experiment by swapping colors out and observing the differences. In the following example of blue, blue-green, and green, any of the three colors could be changed. The blue could be exchanged for a very pale blue, the green for a yellow, or the blue-green for gray or even red. Experiment and note how the relationship between the colors changes. To preserve supplies, allow this stage of inquiry to continue for a while without using scissors and glue. If you've found something that works, set those colors aside and find another combination that also works, perhaps in a different way.

## p012
A little more focus
![p012-f1 545x426](images/2d6102899dbd790e10c6758806522e3118043a4861735a7e56695263a65941ff.jpg)
Beginning the experiment with the white strip allows you to try lots of colors quickly. After you've begun to find combinations that show the phenomenon of color relativity, the effect can be brought into greater focus by placing small pieces of the color that is being made to change its appearance in the center of larger fields of different colors. This example uses blue, blue-green, and green, but you should use whichever colors you've found that work together.
![p012-f2 551x437](images/c424cd0d1020fad9698a8bdbe95685af043bddf433247ace9c765d79dfd63575.jpg)
1. Cut a half-inch strip off one edge of the color that you are attempting to alter in appearance—blue-green in this case.
2. Cut the blue-green strip in half.
3. Place each half in the center of the blue and green papers. The experimenting doesn't need to stop here. These colors can still be swapped out.
![p012-f3 521x393](images/dc3746a4542035ecddfc159c4f0cf5ddb90878078a00b9f88b0208800b295cb8.jpg)
If you're satisfied that you have effectively made one color appear to be two, the entire composition can be glued together. Craft is important! Glue smudges and crooked cuts can distract from the desired effect of seeing the colors change their appearance. In at least one class at Yale, Albers had students inlay the smaller pieces of color into the larger ones. That level of precision isn't always necessary, but it gives an idea of how important a clean presentation was in his classroom.

## p013
[header] Looking together
Josef Albers would frequently announce in the middle of class that it was time for an “exhibition." Each student would bring their best example to the front of class, where the studies were arranged on the floor. Impromptu exhibitions might happen two or three times in one class, as students' experiments became more focused and their ability to talk about what they were seeing became more nuanced. Albers's enthusiasm for interesting combinations was infectious. When talking about student work, he might declare that two colors had gotten together and had babies, or that certain colors were gossiping about a third. These observations emphasized the idea that colors appear in certain ways because of their relationships with other colors, which Albers often compared to human relationships.
If you’re working in a group, gather several finished examples and discuss what you see. If you’re working alone, create three or four examples and compare them to one another. What interactions do you see? Which colors change the most? Are there tendencies or "rules" that seem to guide the behavior of the colors?
A discussion about how the phenomenon works often naturally develops during the initial search for colors. Definitions of several useful terms for talking about how colors appear, including hue, value, saturation, and temperature, can be found in the appendix.

## p014
[header] Factual—Actual
In dealing with color relativity or color illusion, it is practical to distinguish factual facts from actual facts.
![p014-f1 384x676](images/3c6359deaaa9c29adff379478b4998337e81410e2a64aa671b65a9911fd89c3f.jpg)
![p014-f2 387x676](images/289a6d50c916d6054a92844bcb7ee5f679ed4962b49f5d5a66301063664d2719.jpg)
*图注*：Josef Albers, Plate IV-1a from Interaction of Color, 1963.
—JOSEF ALBERS
Identifying the differences between what is quantifiably true (the factual fact) and what is perceived to be true (the actual fact) is a key to understanding Albers's way of seeing color. The factual fact is hat the rectangles in the center of Plate IV-1a are an identical shade of gray. In actual fact—how we perceive them—they appear to be very different: one darker, one lighter; one cooler, one warmer. One may appear to recede, while the other looks like it is coming forward. Perhaps they even seem to be of differing sizes, though with a straightedge you can easily see that they are the same size. What is more, both the sameness and the difference can be simultaneously true. We can hold these conflicting ideas in our minds and contemplate the differences.

## p015
Talking about color studies
Some of the most meaningful visual learning happens while discussing and articulating what we see. If you are working in a classroom setting, bring the gluedtogether studies to a blank wall or a floor area so everyone's work can be seen. Discuss what you see. Point out what is especially surprising in the studies. Having already established some color terms like value, saturation, temperature, and hue, talk about a specific study in terms of those qualities as well as other aspects students may observe. Here is a short commentary on two studies, demonstrating how color terms can be used to discuss the phenomenon of color relativity.
![p015-f1 621x457](images/2770b155cbeb8a4bf70836969259f1df852fb39075e4fd7f761cb5ab2cdae332.jpg)
*图注*：Josef Albers, Plate IV-4a from Interaction of Color, 1963.
![p015-f2 585x445](images/c2204ca0a4f34e99cb6037057a1918501a4d5b6670b91a652d1ff4d735417930.jpg)
*图注*：Josef Albers, Plate IV-3a from Interaction of Color, 1963.
Plate IV-4a comprises two background colors: brown on the left and green on the right. In the foreground, at the center of each is a small rectangle of a second green. Seen against the background green, it is evident that the foreground green is darker (of lower value) than the background green. However, against the brown, the foreground green appears much brighter—almost as bright as the background green on the right. The two greens show a contrast of value, while the brown and foreground green show a contrast of hue. Although the brown and foreground green are different hues, they are approximately the same value. As Albers might say, the two greens are able to talk to one another about hue, while the brown and foreground green are able to talk to one another about value. These affinities and contrasts allow the foreground green to appear very different in relation to the two background colors.
In Plate IV-ga we see that the blue and yellow backgrounds contrast with the superimposed green grid in their hue, but also that other qualities are at play. The yellow is highly saturated, while the green and blue both have some white mixed into them, making them somewhat desaturated and also brighter (higher in value) than they would be if fully saturated. The brightened and desaturated green and blue are closer in value to the yellow than more intense versions would be. Though green will always have something in common with both blue and yellow, the desaturation of green and blue allows the three colors to interact on several levels simultaneously.

## p016
Free studies
You may want to put your newfound color understanding to work right away. Typically, Albers would have students do an exercise in class with strict guidelines—cut out small rectangles of the color to be shifted, and place them directly in the center of the larger areas of background color. Then, as homework, he would ask students to make a free study using the principles they learned in class. Free studies can involve more varied compositions and alternative materials, or may bring in other ideas, so long as they demonstrate the central concept of one (or more) colors changing in appearance.

In-process suggestions
![p017-f1 690x462](images/582fded902a1f24b020e07c597175a455b3f28234a2596b5e7e78e16c3c600b0.jpg)
*图注*：Green goes from dark to light, black moves from the foreground to the background, and blue switches from warm to cool, all depending on which colors are juxtaposed. Four pairs of four different colors give us different quantities of each color and different contrasts.
• Albers avoided definitive rules when talking about color interactions. He was aware of the color wheel but held off talking about it and other color systems until near the end of the course, reasoning that memorizing formulas would keep students from freely experimenting with combinations.
• Though finding a blend of the two background colors may often work (as in Plate IV-ga), more extreme contrasts can be equally effective and even more exciting.
• Working on top of a large piece of white paper makes it easier to focus on your colors, instead of seeing them in relation to the color of your tabletop.
• Looking at lots of colors can be tiring on the eyes. Focusing on the middle of the white strip or the middle of the composition and observing the color interactions in your peripheral vision can make it easier to see the colors shifting and also cause less fatigue.
![p017-f2 690x798](images/1bb0fd90baa9b195a6f11fc26f83fc9bfe7a933734fbc549e8edc35e6d50f80b.jpg)
• Although fully saturated colors can be alluring, muddier colors like tan, pea-green, and gray are sometimes more easily manipulated
• Nothing in the exercise should be considered permanent or precious.   
If one color isn't working, exchange it for another.
• There is no right or wrong combination. The exercise is meant to encourage students to experience color, instead of memorizing specific combinations.
• If you think you have discovered a hard-and-fast rule, try to break the rule.
• Pleasant or harmonious color combinations are not the goal. If you find yourself working only with your favorite colors, find your least favorite color and use it.
• The website www.albersfoundation.org/learning contains videos designed to make this material accessible to younger students.

## p019
With the experience that often we are unsure and thus unable to distinguish between   
lighter and darker in color, it appears appropriate, even necessary, to develop a more   
discriminating sensitivity. —JOSEF ALBERS
![p019-f1 904x965](images/f2eaacbc72a1712531803a1cf55d1f9b513f32e888b9336018dfb6b75741826c.jpg)
*图注*：Snippets cut from newspapers and magazines are arranged to create a smooth gradation from dark gray to light gray.
In both his classroom and Interaction of Color, Albers followed color relativity with gradation studies. Though the two exercises don't seem closely related, it becomes evident when progressing through gradation studies that they are informed by color relativity in interesting ways.
A gradation study is a group of colors that are chosen and arranged so that there is a smooth and sequential transition from one extreme to another.
Value—how light or dark a color appears to be—is the primary quality in question in our initial gradation studies. Since it is most easily observed in grayscale, where we don't have to worry about hue, that is where we begin. It is significantly harder to create a good gradation study between two different hues. We will get there.
Gradations go by many other names: gradient, color scale, ombré, to name a few.   
Albers also referred to them as gray scales and gray ladders.

## p021
In-process suggestions
Working on top of a sheet of white card stock, arrange the collection of grays in order from light to dark, with white at one end and black at the other.
Do any of the grays stand out because they have a slightly warm or cool hue? Remove those.
The difference in appearance between two colors can be called the interval. Because we're only looking at black, white, and gray here, the intervals are based entirely on value. There's no hue to worry about. Two grays that are very similar to one another have a small interval between them. Light gray and dark gray have a much larger interval between them. Gauging intervals is a way to measure the cotrast between colors.
• Creating a gradation study that has equal intervals between each gray can be difficult. Though in a computer program it can be very specifically quantified, what is more important in this exercise is your eye's ability to gauge whether you've made a smooth gradation.
![p021-f1 476x1265](images/b743e107b147898b57fe144089a7c2c9d5b0f3fc6e1b773f7af2d5263fc2bcd1.jpg)
• If a snippet is in question, isolate it along with its neighbors on either side. Does the gray in the middle appear to be halfway between the neighboring grays? If the intervals between each gray are equal, then each gray will be halfway between its neighbors.
• Though more colors are better when collecting for your gradation, you may find that you have many that are quite similar—dark grays, for example. Instead of finding enough middle and light grays to fill the gaps, try editing the dark grays.
Are there any big jumps or gaps in the gradation? You may need to spend more time looking through periodicals for grays to fill those gaps. We want the difference between any two adjacent grays to be equal to that of every other pair of adjacent grays. Maybe you have too many very dark grays and not enough middle grays, which would result in a lot of very small intervals in the dark grays, followed by huge intervals in the middle grays. Ideally the gradation will comprise even intervals all the way from white through the grays to black.
• Look at your gradation from a distance or with squinted eyes. Changing your perspective can help you see the intervals more clearly.
• If you're working with a set of colored paper such as Color-aid, a gradation study of grays may not take very long. Try creating a gradation study within a single hue, such as purple or green, going from light to dark. Avoid looking at the codes on the back of the papers until you've settled on your order! The goal is to learn to trust your eyes.
• If all of your intervals are equal with one exception, that interval may appear to be an angle or bump. Seventeen grays are arranged in order from dark to light in Plate V-ib. Most of the intervals are equal to one another with one exception: the interval between the middle gray and the gray one position darker is larger, which gives the effect of a kink in an otherwise smooth curve.
Making a collection of different values of gray is a valuable use of time, as it allows our eyes to search for and judge colors quickly. After working on the gradation and then returning to the search for more grays, you may notice that your eyes are seeing more subtle variations of gray. This is the goal!
• The two long triangles in Plate V-ib are continuations of the middle gray, though the one next to the lighter grays may look darker and vice versa. Why do you think that is?
![p021-f2 906x162](images/70943169ccafc86aced1fb09f5f4a398448d81ebd8652f9e3cb5d4cced393dc7.jpg)
*图注*：Josef Albers, Plate V-1b from Interaction of Color, 1963
When you are satisfied that the gradation is complete, glue it to the white card stock. This could be done in a very straightforward line or in a more inventive composition, so long as the gradation is evident.

## p022
[header] Gradient inverted
Plate V-1a is a collage of hundreds of snippets of gray, arranged from dark to light. Overlaying the collage is a grid of even gray that matches the middle gray of the gradient. The middle gray grid is something like a window frame. ## p023
More gradation studies—moving between hues
In the case of a gradation between orange and red, the middle color is easily understood to be red-orange. But what is halfway between orange and green? Albers asks us, when looking for the halfway point between two colors, to envision the optical mixture of two colors. That is different from the physical mixture. If you were to mix equal parts of orange and green paint, the result would be a brown, but it probably would not appear to be exactly halfway between orange and green—one of the hues would likely dominate. Instead, and since we're using colored paper, not paint, we are looking for a color that has equal parts of both orange and green. There's a very good chance that it's a muddy color, probably a brown gray. It will have green and orange in it, too, and when placed between them, it will be convincing that it is halfway between the two. This is easier said than done!
When attempting to create a gradation study between hues, you may find it more effective to work with a color set like Color-aid.
Though Albers was primarily focused on value when he introduced gradation studies, there is much to be gained from creating a gradation between hues. Could you create a smooth gradation—one in which each interval is equal— between orange and red? How about between green and orange?

The benefit of this challenging search is the new focus it gives our eyes. We become very tuned in to the intervals between colors. An ability to observe and control the intervals between colors is vital to many of Albers's exercises.

How can this be? The simultaneous contrast that we observed in chapter IV, on color relativity, is certainly at play, but the situation with reversed grounds is a bit more complicated. One color is not just taking on two different appearances in relation to two different backgrounds; in this case one color is becoming the two distinctly different colors of the background. It appears that the small rectangles are the same colors as the backgrounds on the opposite side. There seem to be only two colors present, a dark blue and a light blue, but in fact three colors have been made to look like two.
What is the relationship between the three blues? Or asked in another way: What characteristics would the blue in the small rectangles need to have in order to simultaneously appear to be both the light blue and the dark blue? The answer: The blue of the small rectangles is the middle mixture of the dark and light blues. It contains equal parts of both dark and light blue.

## p033
If we stare for half a minute at the red circle, focusing continually on the marked center,   
and then focus on the white center, we do not see white. The color we see there is the   
after-image of red. —JOSEF ALBERS
![p033-f1 901x576](images/eb4a150882a1c4299623e1e6add79e6cedab4b2c8b5ccdbd99133dce018f7cb4.jpg)
*图注*：Josef Albers, Plate VIII-1a from Interaction of Color, 1963.
Focus your eyes for thirty seconds on the black dot at the center of the red circle. Hold your eyes there, just looking at the black dot. After thirty seconds shift your eyes to the black dot in the center of the white circle. What do you see?
After-image informs our understanding of color in a very different way from color relativity. Albers explains that the after-image phenomenon is caused by the physiology of the human eye. Light is registered by photoreceptors, known as rods and cones, that line the backs of our retinas. When the photoreceptors that register red are exposed to the same strong red for thirty seconds, as Albers has just asked us to do, the receptors become fatigued. Meanwhile, we look away. The brain, however, continues to receive photoreceptor signals as if the red circle was still present. During the few seconds it takes for the fatigued photoreceptors to refresh themselves, our brain receives “false information," and we see an afterimage color. For most people, the after-image of red is blue-green.
Albers used the terms after-image and simultaneous contrast interchangeably. Simultaneous contrast is most often understood to be a description of the tendency of adjacent colors to shift in appearance relative to the colors around them (read more about simultaneous contrast in the appendix), and so we will only use the term after-image here.

## p047
Illusions of transparence and depth in use
Even more than in previous exercises, creating the illusion of transparence with colored paper requires being able to see and control the intervals between colors. For Albers it represented an articulation of clear thinking that might be applied in painting, collage, other studio practices, or far beyond. He compared the lessons of his color course to learning grammar: one must first grasp the basics before attempting to read or write poetry.' An ability to see and control the intervals between colors to create illusions of transparence and depth is itself poetic and can be transposed into countless other modes of thinking and making.
![p047-f1 901x906](images/ac24d3b6438b6fa8aa19493e2404091e32f47fac132da55d410197268aeb8295.jpg)
*图注*：Josef Albers, Study for Homage to the Square: Lone Whites, 1963. Oil on Masonite, 24 × 24 in. (61 × 61 cm). The Josef and Anni Albers Foundation,
Albers's 1963 painting Homage to the Square: Lone Whites comprises a square of white nested within two slightly darker squares of gray. It appears as though a square of tissue has been laid atop the center of the painting, extending beyond and diffusing a light emanating from the central square. It's as if there are only two colors of paint—the central white and the outer gray—while the middle gray is only the result of the tissue. Employing an illusion of transparence was not uncommon in Albers's Homage to the Square series, as well as in others of his works. As with nearly all of his paintings, Albers applied each of the three colors separately, without mixing or overpainting.º He achieved the illusion of a translucent tissue by finding a gray that was halfway between the central white and the outer darker gray, similar to the first example in this chapter.

Colors and hues are defined, as are tones in music, by wave length. —JOSEF ALBERS
Hum (out loud or in your head) the first notes of "Happy Birthday." "Happy birthday to you" comprises four different musical notes. They could be sung in any voice and in any key. We recognize it as the tune of "Happy Birthday" not because of an individual note, but because of the relationship between the notes. Albers calls this relationship the “acoustical constellation" of the tune. We will recognize it whether the first note is an A or a C# or any other note, so long as the following notes have the correct intervals.
The same can be true of a group of colors. The relationship of the four pinks is identical to the relationship between the four blues. This means that the interval between the dark pink in the upper left and the pale pink in the upper right is equal to the interval between the dark blue in the upper left and pale blue in the upper right. The relationship of the two bottom pinks and the two bottom blues is also equal. The same is true for the two left pinks and two left blues, and the two right pinks and two right blues. If the four pinks are a group of musical notes, they are transposed in their exact orientation to the key of blue.

Color systems [such as Munsell's, Ostwald's, and others] usually lead to the conclusion that certain constellations within a system provide color harmony. They indicate that this is mainly the aim and the end of color combination, of color juxtaposition. —JOSEF ALBERS
The idea that certain colors “go together" or can always be counted on to create harmony was antithetical to Albers's much more open-eyed approach. Albers was interested in the malleability of color. To understand color is to be aware of its ever-changing nature.
To better illuminate the malleability of color, Albers created an exercise based on color quantity. His conclusion is that "any color 'goes' or 'works' with any other color, presupposing that their quantities are appropríate."
Each of the four compositions on the previous page uses the same four colors— dark blue, light blue, pink, and brown—but in very different quantities and dispersions. Not only do the colors work in harmony, but each composition also appears very different from the other three.

Albers also called this exercise the Four Color Worlds, highlighting the idea that students should make each of the four compositions appear to be completely its own world.
The basic parameters can be shifted as needed. In general they are:
• Make four compositions using four colors.
• Each composition will include all four colors.
• Each composition will employ the same visual motif (vertical stripes, circles, triangles, or something else).
• The goal is to make the color impact of each composition feel as different as possible from the other three.

Albers moved from Germany to the United States in 1933, when he accepted a teaching position at the newly formed Black Mountain College in western North Carolina. Compared to the mostly brown autumn foliage of northern Europe, the explosion of yellows, oranges, and reds in the Blue Ridge Mountain forests in autumn was a revelation for Albers. Black Mountain College was, moreover, perpetually underfunded, especially during the Great Depression years. But leaves were abundant and cost nothing. They were a welcome addition to the colored paper that Albers's students customarily used.
With slight or intense modulations, color is rarely homogenous throughout a given leaf, making them a difficult material to use for some of the earlier exercises in this book. For a free study, however, leaves present innumerable tints, shades, and shapes for inventive design. There are countless ways to begin and directions to take in a leaf study. What follows is meant to provide some possible first ideas, but should by no means limit you. Nor do you need to live in a place where the leaves change colors. Green leaves, brown leaves, or any other object from nature could be incorporated.

## p065
Leaf creatures
Younger (and older) audiences may enjoy creating creatures from the shapes and colors they find in leaves. There is no right or wrong way to go about this. A single leaf might suggest the shape of an animal's body or head, which can be augmented with additional leaves or parts of leaves. Albers sometimes added paint to his leaf studies. ## p066
APPENDIX
If you are working in a classroom or other group setting, a discussion about how the phenomena of color perception work often naturally develops during the initial search for colors. Drawing out a few key terms is helpful so that you can talk about what you're seeing.

## p067
Key terms for talking about color
Contrast simply means that something—a color in our case—is different from another thing. There can be a large contrast between two colors, as between black and white, or a small contrast, as between purple and violet.
![p067-f1 900x603](images/672144da99158396389a5d79a16e904f71306badca822392c2cdb7106a0c253f.jpg)
Red, orange, yellow, green, blue, and purple are the different hues of color. Hue refers to the name of a color. Red and green, for example, are two colors that contrast in hue. The color wheel, which is discussed later in this section, comprises all of the hues.
Typically we think of the warm colors as being yellow, orange, and red, while the cool colors are green, blue, and purple. A contrast of temperature can be achieved by placing a cool color next to a warm color.
Saturation is another way of talking about how intense or strong a color is. A color that is weaker or less intense is desaturated. Colors that are desaturated have some white or black mixed into them. The red on a stop sign is very saturated. Pink is an example of a desaturated color, because pink is red that has some white mixed into it. A very light pink is less intense than a fully saturated red, and so a contrast can be seen when the two are placed next to one another.
The phenomenon of color relativity is an example of simultaneous contrast, in which the contrast of colors on one side of the composition affects how we perceive the contrast on the other, and vice versa. Simultaneous contrast was discussed at length by the nineteenth-century French chemist Michel-Eugène Chevreul, but British philosopher John Locke, among others, remarked upon the phenomenon as far back as the seventeenth century. Albers's explication of the concept has proven especially accessible. Confusingly, Albers used the term simultaneous contrast interchangeably with after-image, a term addressed in chapter VIII. To clarify, we will limit the use of simultaneous contrast in this book to refer to color relativity.
Value is a measure of how light or dark a color is. Bright yellow and white are extremely high in value because they are so light. Deep purple and dark green are examples of colors with low value. Contrast between high and low value colors can be easily seen. Two colors that are of similar value can be harder to differentiate because of their low contrast. In more specific terms value is a measure of how much light is bouncing off a surface and landing in your eye. For that reason a color's value can change in different lighting situations and can be different for two people at different places in the same room.

## p068
The color wheel
![p068-f1 903x610](images/8dfdf8cc906d35e8a1bf679d010a73ba7955f6051ed40df3120627cdf3c72d06.jpg)
*图注*：Josef Albers discussing color temperature, Yale University, 1955-56. Photo by John Cohen.
Albers wanted his students to experience color firsthand without the need to memorize color systems such as the color wheel. Ideally, students would be able to leave previous knowledge about or preferences for colors behind. In my own experiences of teaching this material, I try to steer the conversation away from the color wheel while students explore color relativity. A student might suggest that to create a contrast of hues, you must use complementary colors—colors that sit across from one another on the color wheel. I might respond by agreeing, but add that while orange and blue do create a very high contrast, so do orange and green, or orange and purple—or, if saturation levels are changed, you can create a dramatic contrast between orange and red.
Because Albers was interested in the perception of color as pigments—paint or colored paper—the color wheel can be a useful reference. A photograph of Albers teaching at Yale University in the 195os shows him dividing the color wheel according to temperature, with red, orange, and yellow named as warm colors, while purple, blue, and green are defined as cool colors. Finding the middle mixture of two colors can be conceptualized with a color wheel.
Colors have been organized into circles or wheels for centuries, going back at least to British scientist Isaac Newton, who first published a color wheel in his book Opticks in 17o4. Newton and others before him knew that colored light behaves differently than colored pigment. The visible wavelengths of colored light—red, orange, yellow, green, blue, and purple—are most easily organized in a straight line, with red at one end and purple on the other. Newton put the colored pigments in the same order as colored light, and since it is commonly understood that red and yellow pigment mix to make orange, yellow and blue mix to make green, and blue and red mix to make purple, connecting purple to red in the color wheel made logical sense. There is much more to be said about the history and physics of the color wheel, but for our purposes, let's accept that it exists and comprises six hues in clockwise order: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, purple, and back to red.
The color wheel also aids in understanding after-image. As we learned in chapter VIII, every color has an after-image color. Each color's after-image is its opposite on the color wheel. Blue's after-image is orange; orange's is blue; yellow's is purple; purple's is yellow, and so on. But why is red's after-image blue-green and not just green? This gets into complicated physics and physiology. Although we're looking at colored pigment, what is registering in our eyes is light reflecting off that pigment. In this case color is behaving both as a pigment and as light. A more nuanced color wheel or one made of colored light (for instance, on a screen) could correct for this. The wheel of six equal wedges of color presented here is a useful simplification.

## p069
The color globe
With these guideposts encircling our globe, we can with good accuracy find the location of any color we can imagine. What's more, if we locate any two colors, we can draw a line between them, and halfway down that line find the color that is the mixture of the two endpoints.
![p069-f1 443x443](images/6903af39bc243df2795de55ea08424047839f2c4fc3d32640e6d74b881052d6d.jpg)
*图注*：The diagonal line overlaying this color globe shows the location of orange-white, bluegreen, and a greenish color that is the halfway point or middle mixture of orange-white and blue-green
Picture a translucent globe. Instead of a surface map of a planet, it is a threedimensional depiction of all of the colors visible to the human eye. Around the equator are the fully saturated hues of red, orange, yellow, green, blue, and purple—the color wheel. The north pole is pure white, and the south pole is pure black. Moving closer to the axis on a cross-section cut through the equator, each hue becomes more and more muddy and gray.
Parallel to the equator and halfway to each pole is another circle around the globe. The circle between the equator and the white north pole is another color wheel, but in this one each hue has been desaturated with white. Red becomes pink, blue becomes blue-white, orange becomes orange-white, and so on.
The circle halfway between the equator and the black south pole is another color wheel, but here each hue has been desaturated with black. Red becomes maroon, blue becomes blue-black, orange becomes orange-black, and so on.
In the early twentieth century, the German chemist Friedrich WilheIm Ostwald created a color system organized as a transparent globe. Albers discusses Ostwald's color system and other color spaces in chapter XXIV of Interaction of Color.
